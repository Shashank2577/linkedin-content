## Post body (copy from here)

OpenAI gave four outside labs access to its source code, model checkpoints and training data on November 16.

The useful part is that the labs can publish what they find.

The four are Apollo Research, METR, Transluce and Goodfire, and each gets a different slice: Apollo spends four months inside OpenAI studying whether training rewards subversion, while Goodfire embeds researchers to monitor training runs and debug alignment. Transluce runs a six-week pilot with raw chains of thought and non-public evals. METR runs two assessments on alignment and monitoring. Transluce plans an eight-week follow-on next year.

Two details make this more than a press release. Apollo and Goodfire are paid, but OpenAI says the pay isn't contingent on results. METR and Transluce take no money, though they may accept compute or API credits. And the evaluators can publish findings, with a dispute process if OpenAI disagrees.

The limitation is scope. Transluce says its access is not employee-level, despite OpenAI's "deep access" framing. Six weeks is a pilot, not continuous oversight. That's the gap. I've read a lot of these announcements, and this one has more teeth than most.

Sam Altman promised "employee-like access" in September, after Dario Amodei at Anthropic made a similar pledge. Anthropic has since signed Accenture and METR. The race is on.

For anyone building on these models, this is the closest thing to an audit trail that exists today. It's still the vendor choosing the auditors. But publishable findings beat a system card written by the same team that built the model. I'd take that trade.
